# A city without mail

*How communication shaped [[Käte Lazarus]]'s 1948 experience.*

## 1 May — a letter that cannot leave

Käte says a reply written by “Dad” is still in Jerusalem because the post is not going out. She sees her inability to communicate with the children and grandchildren as the hardest aspect of isolation (letters PDF p.2).

## 5 May — an old letter arrives

She receives a letter written 16 April, after an 18-day delay. She is profoundly moved by its words and describes the relief of reading it (p.7).

## 13 May — tentative experiments

Käte discusses letters carried by Jewish planes to the coastal plain. The family has written to Walter and Kurt, but she says there has been no reply for weeks (p.13).

## Late May — finding intermediaries

She tries messages through diplomatic contacts and acquaintances, and reports occasional incoming mail from the United States while still lacking news of the children (pp.22–23).

## 4–12 June — improvisation

The letter has already grown into a multiweek document because she cannot send it. By 12 June she discusses various methods of getting short notes to Tel Aviv, including with travelers (pp.25, 29).

## 15–23 June — a fragile connection returns

A telegram brings news that family members are safe (p.31). On 23 June, Käte says the Jerusalem postal service is functioning again and considers sending the pages of her diary by registered airmail; she reports receiving several recent letters from the family (pp.34–35).

## A central open question

Who was the intended addressee or addressees? The cover doesn't name them. Käte writes about **“אבא”** as someone alongside her in Jerusalem, and several letters address recipients in ways that suggest female family members or a couple. **Do not identify Arthur as the recipient** unless the German originals or envelopes confirm it.
