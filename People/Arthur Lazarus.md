# Arthur Lazarus

**28 January 1886 – 3 January 1957**, as printed on the memoir cover (PDF p.1).

## Childhood and family

Arthur was born in [[Stargard]], Pomerania. His parents were [[Aron Uriel Lazarus]] and [[Henriette Gitel Lazarus]]. His mother died in 1893, after which Arthur spent part of his childhood with relatives [[Aron and Bertha Will]]; he later reflected openly on the affection he felt he had lacked at home (memoir PDF pp.2–3).

He married [[Käte Lazarus]] on **2 September 1913**. Their wedding program and portrait survive (memoir pp.6–7). Their three children were [[Walter Lazarus]], [[Kurt Lazarus]], and [[Brigitte Lazarus]].

## Experiences

- Arthur was mobilized in 1915 and spent much of World War I away from Käte and baby Walter. His memoir speaks strikingly little about combat and more about separation from his family (memoir pp.8–9); see [[The young family in World War I]].
- He prospered as a businessman. In 1921 he bought land in [[Dahlem]], where the family lived from December 1922 until their departure from Germany; see [[Life in Dahlem]] (pp.14–16).
- **Important corrected chronology:** After the Nazi rise in **1933**, Arthur and Käte planned emigration and inspected Palestine in September 1933. He dates their departure from Germany to **November 1936**, not 1933 (memoir p.17; [[Departure from Germany]]).
- The family first settled in [[Tel Aviv]], then Arthur and Käte moved to [[Jerusalem]], where he says they lived **1941–1949** (memoir p.17).
- His memoir describes siege-related food and water shortages in Jerusalem, and a later move to [[Shaar Hefer]] encouraged by their sons (memoir p.18).

## Personality and voice

Arthur's memoir is not simply a celebratory family record. He writes with pride about the garden and home in Dahlem (p.15), acknowledges fears and doubts surrounding emigration (p.17), and ends with a mixture of gratitude for his family's survival and harsh criticism of the young state's economic direction (p.19). These are *his views, written in 1953*, and shouldn't be presented as objective assessments of historical events.

He closes with a wish not to become a burden on his children (memoir p.20).
